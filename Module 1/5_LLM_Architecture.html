Section 01

What Makes a Language Model "Large"?

A language model is nothing more than a probability distribution over the next token, conditioned on everything that came before it: P(token_t | token_1, ..., token_{t-1}). Every LLM — GPT-4, Claude, Llama — is this one idea, scaled up by orders of magnitude in parameters, data, and compute.

The lineage: three generations of language models

Generation How it estimates P(next token | context) Context limit Weakness
N-gram (1990s) Count co-occurrence frequencies in a corpus, back off to smaller n on unseen sequences 2–5 tokens (fixed window) Sparse — most n-grams never appear in training data
RNN/LSTM LM (2010s) Compress all prior tokens into one hidden state, updated sequentially Unbounded in theory, ~dozens of tokens in practice Long-range signal decays; sequential, can't parallelize training
Transformer LM (2017–) Self-attention lets every token directly weigh every prior token Thousands to millions of tokens O(n²) memory; needs massive data + compute to reach fluency
"Large" refers to three things scaling together: parameter count (millions → hundreds of billions), training data (gigabytes → many trillions of tokens), and compute (GPU-hours). Kaplan et al. (2020) and Hoffmann et al. (2022, "Chinchilla") showed these three must scale together — more parameters alone, without proportionally more data, wastes compute. See §07.

The training objective: causal language modeling

// Autoregressive next-token prediction — the only objective pretraining needs Loss = - Σ_t log P(token_t | token_1, ..., token_{t-1}; θ) // Concretely, for the sentence "the cat sat down": P(the) // no context — just the prior over first tokens P(cat | the) P(sat | the, cat) P(down | the, cat, sat) // Every token in every document becomes a free training example. // No human labels required — this is why pretraining scales to trillions of tokens.

This objective is self-supervised: the "label" for predicting token 5 is just token 5 itself, already present in the raw text. That's the entire trick that makes it possible to pretrain on the open internet without paying for annotation.

Measuring a language model: perplexity

// Perplexity = how "surprised" the model is by held-out text, on average PPL = exp( -1/N · Σ log P(token_t | context) ) // Intuition: PPL = 1 → model predicts the correct next token with certainty // PPL = |V| → model is as clueless as uniform random guessing over the vocabulary // GPT-2 (2019): ~35 PPL on WebText. Modern frontier LLMs: single digits on curated benchmarks.
Why this matters for everything downstream: chat, coding, reasoning, tool-use — every LLM capability you interact with is built on top of this single next-token objective, plus post-training (instruction tuning, RLHF, and the fine-tuning techniques covered in Module 6). The base capability is entirely a byproduct of getting extremely good at predicting the next token across a vast and varied corpus.
Section 02

Tokenization — Turning Text into Numbers

Neural networks only consume numbers. Before any embedding or attention happens, raw text must be chopped into discrete units — tokens — and each token mapped to an integer ID. The choice of tokenization scheme has real, visible consequences on model behavior.

Why not just use words, or just characters?

Whole-word tokenization
  • Vocabulary explodes: every inflection ("run", "running", "runner") is a separate ID
  • Any word not seen in training → <UNK>, information lost
  • Cannot generalize to typos, new words, or other languages
Character-level tokenization
  • Tiny vocabulary (~100 symbols), no <UNK> problem
  • Sequences become 4–5× longer for the same text
  • Self-attention is O(n²) — 5× the tokens costs ~25× the compute
The middle ground: subword tokenization. Keep common whole words as single tokens ("the", "cat") but break rare/long words into reusable pieces ("token" + "ization"). This gives a bounded vocabulary (30k–250k tokens), no <UNK>s, and reasonable sequence lengths. Every modern LLM uses some form of this.

Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) — worked example

BPE builds its vocabulary bottom-up: start from individual characters, and repeatedly merge the most frequent adjacent pair into a new token.

// Toy corpus (word, frequency): "low"×5, "lower"×2, "newest"×6, "widest"×3 // Start: every word split into characters + end-of-word marker "_" l o w _ × 5 l o w e r _ × 2 n e w e s t _ × 6 w i d e s t _ × 3 // Merge 1: most frequent adjacent pair is (e, s) → appears in "newest"×6 + "widest"×3 = 9 times es becomes a new token → n e w es t _ | w i d es t _ // Merge 2: (es, t) now appears 9 times → merge to "est" n e w est _ | w i d est _ // Merge 3: (l, o) appears in "low"×5 + "lower"×2 = 7 times → merge to "lo" lo w _ | lo w e r _ // Continue for a fixed number of merges (e.g. 30,000) → final vocabulary
Scheme Used by How it picks merges
BPE GPT-2/3/4, Llama (byte-level variant) Greedy: merge the most frequent adjacent pair
WordPiece BERT Merge the pair that most increases training-data likelihood, not just raw frequency
SentencePiece (Unigram) T5, Gemma, Llama (as the training framework) Start from a large candidate vocabulary, prune tokens that least hurt overall likelihood
Known tokenizer artifacts (classic interview questions): LLMs are famously bad at counting letters in a word ("how many r's in strawberry?") because strawberry is often a single token or split as straw+ berry — the model never sees individual characters, only opaque token IDs. Similarly, arithmetic on large numbers is hard because digit grouping into tokens is inconsistent ( "1234" might tokenize differently than "11234", breaking place-value patterns the model would otherwise learn).

Vocabulary size is a real design trade-off

Vocabulary size Sequence length for given text Embedding table size Example
Small (~32k) Longer sequences Smaller (fewer rows) Llama 2 (32k)
Large (~250k) Shorter sequences, better multilingual coverage Larger — can be 10%+ of total params in small models Gemma (256k), GPT-4 (~200k)
Section 03

Embeddings — From Token IDs to Meaning

A token ID (e.g. 4521) carries no meaning by itself — it's an arbitrary index. The embedding layer is a lookup table that converts each ID into a dense vector of learned numbers, which does carry meaning, shaped entirely by training.

FIG 1 — Left: the full pipeline from raw text to a lookup in the embedding matrix. Right: the classic vector-arithmetic property of learned embedding spaces (illustrative, projected to 2D — real embeddings live in hundreds of dimensions).

Key properties of the embedding layer

1

It's just a matrix

Shape |V| × d_model. Looking up token ID 4521 is literally reading row 4521. No computation, pure indexing.

2

Learned, not designed

Initialized randomly, updated by backprop like every other weight. Meaning emerges purely from the next-token prediction objective.

3

Often tied to the output layer

Many LLMs reuse the same matrix (transposed) as the final vocabulary-projection layer — "weight tying" — saving a large chunk of parameters.

4

Dominant parameter cost at small scale

For a 256k-vocabulary, 2048-dim model, the embedding matrix alone is ~500M parameters — significant for a small LLM.

What the embedding layer does NOT do: it has no notion of word order — "cat sat" and "sat cat" produce the same two embedding vectors, just possibly in a different sequence. Order is injected separately — that's the whole subject of the next section.
Section 04

Positional Encoding, Revisited — and How Modern LLMs Actually Do It

Module 4 introduced sinusoidal positional encoding, added once to the input embeddings. Modern production LLMs mostly use something else. This section is the deeper dive: what changed, and why.

Scheme How position enters the model Used by Extrapolates past training length?
Sinusoidal (absolute) Fixed sin/cos pattern added to input embeddings once, at the bottom Original Transformer (2017) Poorly
Learned absolute A trainable embedding table indexed by position, added to input BERT, GPT-2 No — undefined beyond max trained length
RoPE (Rotary) Rotates Q and K vectors by an angle proportional to position, inside every attention layer Llama, Mistral, Gemma, GPT-NeoX, most modern open LLMs Reasonably, with extensions (e.g. NTK-aware scaling)
ALiBi No positional vectors at all — instead, subtracts a distance-proportional penalty directly from attention scores BLOOM, MPT Very well — trains short, tests long

Rotary Position Embeddings (RoPE) — the modern default

What is RoPE?

RoPE is a way of telling the attention mechanism "how far apart are these two tokens" without ever adding a position vector to the embedding. Every other scheme on the table above works by injecting a position signal — adding a sinusoid or a learned vector onto the token embedding before it enters the network. RoPE does something different: it leaves the embedding alone and instead rotates the query and key vectors, inside the attention layer, by an angle that grows with each token's position. The content of the vector doesn't change (rotation preserves length), only its direction does — and that rotation angle is where positional information now lives.

How it works

Pick a fixed angle-per-step, θ (e.g. 10° per position, as in FIG 2 below). Before computing attention, every query vector at position i gets rotated by i·θ, and every key vector at position j gets rotated by j·θ. The two vectors involved in a single attention score — one query, one key — arrive at the dot product each carrying their own rotation. Because a rotation is orthogonal (it preserves angles and lengths), the dot product of two rotated vectors depends only on the difference between the two rotation angles, i.e. on i − j. The absolute positions themselves cancel out. That's the whole mechanism: two absolute rotations combine, at dot-product time, into one relative signal.

FIG 2 — RoPE rotates each query/key vector by an angle proportional to its position. Because rotation is applied to both Q and K before their dot product, the score naturally encodes relative distance.

The formula

In the simplified 2D case (real RoPE splits the full d_k-dimensional head into pairs of dimensions and applies this same rotation to every pair, each pair with its own θ), the rotation applied to a vector x = (x₁, x₂) at position pos is:

// R(φ) is a standard 2D rotation matrix, φ = pos·θ RoPE(x, pos) = R(pos·θ)·x = (x₁cos(pos·θ) − x₂sin(pos·θ), x₁sin(pos·θ) + x₂cos(pos·θ)) // applied to q at position i and k at position j, then dotted together: RoPE(q, i) · RoPE(k, j) = f(q, k, i − j) // provably depends only on the relative distance i−j, never on i or j alone

Worked example — applying the formula exactly: "the cat sat down"

Take the same toy sentence used in the causal-mask example later in this module — the cat sat down — tokenized at positions 0, 1, 2, 3. The query at "sat" (position 2) attends back to the keys at "cat" (position 1) and "the" (position 0). Use θ = 10° per step, and give every token the same toy 2D content vector x = (1, 0) before rotation, so any difference in the final scores comes purely from position, not content.

Step 1 — rotate each vector using the formula above. With x = (1, 0), the formula (x₁cos φ − x₂sin φ, x₁sin φ + x₂cos φ) simplifies to (cos φ, sin φ) since x₂ = 0:

// φ = pos·θ, plugged directly into RoPE(x, pos) = (x₁cosφ − x₂sinφ, x₁sinφ + x₂cosφ) k("the", pos 0): φ = 0×10° = 0° → (cos0° − 0·sin0°, 0·cos0° + sin0°) = (1.000, 0.000) k("cat", pos 1): φ = 1×10° = 10° → (cos10° − 0·sin10°, 0·cos10° + sin10°) = (0.985, 0.174) q("sat", pos 2): φ = 2×10° = 20° → (cos20° − 0·sin20°, 0·cos20° + sin20°) = (0.940, 0.342)

Step 2 — score each pair with the plain dot product q·k = q₁k₁ + q₂k₂, using the numbers from Step 1:

q("sat")·k("cat") = 0.940×0.985 + 0.342×0.174 ≈ 0.985 q("sat")·k("the") = 0.940×1.000 + 0.342×0.000 ≈ 0.940

Step 3 — check it against the formula's guarantee. Because every rotated vector is (cos φ, sin φ), the dot product q₁k₁ + q₂k₂ is exactly the trig identity cos(A)cos(B) + sin(A)sin(B) = cos(A − B), where A is the query's angle and B is the key's angle:

q("sat")·k("cat") = cos(20°)cos(10°) + sin(20°)sin(10°) = cos(20°−10°) = cos(10°) ≈ 0.985 // gap = 1 position q("sat")·k("the") = cos(20°)cos(0°) + sin(20°)sin(0°) = cos(20°−0°) = cos(20°) ≈ 0.940 // gap = 2 positions // general rule proven above: q(pos_i)·k(pos_j) = cos((pos_i − pos_j)·θ) — depends only on the gap

Step 2 and Step 3 are not two different calculations — they are the same sum, computed the long way (raw components) and the short way (the identity). Every score you get from Step 1's numbers will match Step 3's cos(gap·θ) exactly, because Step 1's vectors are, by construction, points on the unit circle at angle pos·θ. In the "sat"–"the" row, k("the") = (1, 0) sits at angle 0°, so sin(0°) = 0 silently kills the second term of the dot product — that's why the Step 2 sum reduces to a single multiplication, not a different rule.

The practical payoff: it wouldn't matter whether "sat" and "cat" sat at positions (2, 1) or (52, 51) in a much longer document — as long as the gap stays 1, RoPE contributes the exact same positional score, cos(10°) ≈ 0.985. And it explains why "sat" gets a higher raw positional score toward its immediate neighbour "cat" (gap 1 → cos 10° ≈ 0.985) than toward the more distant "the" (gap 2 → cos 20° ≈ 0.940): the rotation angle grows with distance, so cosine — which shrinks as its angle grows — gives nearby tokens a mild positional boost before content similarity is even factored in.

Exam favourite: "Why do most modern open-weight LLMs use RoPE instead of the original sinusoidal PE?" — RoPE bakes relative position directly into the attention dot product itself (no separate addition step), integrates cleanly with KV-caching for fast inference, and empirically extrapolates to longer sequences better than absolute schemes.
Section 05

Decoder-Only Architecture — What Actually Powers GPT, Llama & Claude

Module 4 covered the full encoder–decoder Transformer. But almost every LLM you use day-to-day — GPT-4, Claude, Llama, Gemini, Mistral — is decoder-only: there is no separate encoder, no cross-attention. Just a stack of causal self-attention blocks, repeated.

Why drop the encoder entirely?

The original encoder–decoder design exists for tasks with two distinct sequences (source language → target language). But a huge fraction of useful tasks — chat, completion, summarization, code generation — can be reframed as one long sequence: "instruction + context + answer," all predicted left-to-right. If everything is one sequence, you don't need an encoder/decoder split at all — just one stack that attends causally over everything so far.

FIG 3 — The causal (lower-triangular) mask used in every decoder-only LLM layer: each token can only attend to itself and earlier tokens.

Inference: one token at a time, with a KV cache

Training processes a whole sequence in parallel. Generation is fundamentally different — the model produces one token, appends it to the input, and repeats. Naively, this means recomputing K and V for every past token at every single new step — hugely wasteful. The fix: cache them.

// Naive generation (no cache) — recompute everything every step step 1: K,V for [the] → predict "cat" step 2: K,V for [the, cat] (recomputed!) → predict "sat" step 3: K,V for [the, cat, sat] (recomputed!) → predict "down" // With a KV cache — reuse what's already been computed step 1: compute K,V for [the] → cache them → predict "cat" step 2: compute K,V for [cat] ONLY → append to cache → predict "sat" step 3: compute K,V for [sat] ONLY → append to cache → predict "down" // Cost per new token drops from O(n²) recomputation to O(n) — this is why // "context length" directly determines how much GPU memory inference needs: // cache size = 2 × n_layers × n_heads × d_head × seq_len × 2 (K and V) × batch
Exam favourite: "Why does inference get slower and use more memory as the conversation gets longer?" — the KV cache grows linearly with every token generated; it must hold K and V for every previous token, in every layer, for the whole conversation.

From logits to a token — temperature, top-k, top-p

The last layer of the decoder doesn't output a word. It outputs one raw score — a logit — for every entry in the vocabulary: tens of thousands of numbers, unbounded, not yet a probability. Turning that into one chosen token is a separate step called decoding, and unlike everything else in this module, it's controlled by parameters you set at inference time — not by trained weights. Here is exactly what happens, in order, before a token is picked:

// step-by-step: raw model output → one sampled token 1. logits = final_layer(hidden_state) // one score per vocab entry, e.g. [−2.1, 4.7, 0.3, ...] 2. logits' = logits / T // TEMPERATURE — reshape before softmax 3. probs = softmax(logits') // now a valid distribution: all ≥ 0, sums to 1 4. probs' = filter(probs, top_k / top_p) // TOP-K / TOP-P — cut which tokens are even eligible 5. probs'' = renormalize(probs') // what's left is rescaled to sum to 1 again 6. token = sample(probs'') // or argmax(probs'') if T→0 — fully greedy, deterministic

Temperature (T) rescales the logits before softmax: P(token_i) = exp(logit_i / T) / Σⱼ exp(logit_j / T). Dividing by a small T (e.g. 0.3) stretches the gaps between logits apart, so softmax pushes almost all the probability mass onto the single highest-scoring token — at the limit, T→0 is identical to always taking argmax (greedy decoding: fully deterministic, same output every run). Dividing by a large T (e.g. 1.6) compresses the gaps toward zero, so softmax flattens toward a near-uniform distribution — low-probability tokens get a real shot at being picked, which reads as more "creative" but also less reliable. T = 1 leaves the model's own distribution untouched.

Worked example — same 4 logits, four temperatures. Take raw logits [2, 3, 4, 1] (logit 4 is the max, so it wins at every T — the question is only how decisively). Divide by T, then apply softmax:

// logits/T, then softmax — same formula as above, T = 0.1, 0.4, 0.5, 0.7 logits = [2, 3, 4, 1] // T = 0.1 — divide by 0.1 = multiply gaps by 10× → 40−30 = 10-point gap before exp() logits/T = [20, 30, 40, 10] → softmax = [ ~0.0000, 0.00005, 0.99995, ~0.0000] // T = 0.4 logits/T = [5, 7.5, 10, 2.5] → softmax = [ 0.0062, 0.0754, 0.9180, 0.0005] // T = 0.5 logits/T = [4, 6, 8, 2] → softmax = [ 0.0158, 0.1171, 0.8649, 0.0021] // T = 0.7 logits/T = [2.857, 4.286, 5.714, 1.429] → softmax = [ 0.0438, 0.1828, 0.7629, 0.0105] // as T rises from 0.1 → 0.7, P(logit=4) falls 0.99995 → 0.9180 → 0.8649 → 0.7629 — // the winner never changes, only how much mass everything else is allowed to take from it
FIG 4 — The same six-candidate next-token distribution after "The cat sat on the ___", reshaped by each decoding control in turn. A→B: temperature changes the distribution itself, before any filtering. C, D: top-k and top-p don't reshape probabilities at all — they cut which tokens are eligible, then renormalize what's left. Note top-k always keeps exactly 3 tokens regardless of shape; top-p keeps as many as needed to reach 90% cumulative mass — 5 here, because this particular raw distribution isn't very peaked. Grey bars = excluded (probability forced to 0).

Top-k keeps only the k highest-probability tokens and renormalizes just those before sampling — a fixed count, regardless of how the distribution is shaped. Top-p (nucleus sampling) instead keeps the smallest set of tokens whose cumulative probability is ≥ p, then renormalizes — a fixed mass, so the count it keeps adapts automatically: very few tokens when the model is confident (one token already near p on its own), many more when it's genuinely uncertain. This is why top-p is generally preferred over top-k in production systems — it doesn't force the same cutoff width onto every prediction.

Worked example — top-k and top-p on the same distribution. Reuse the T = 0.5 result from above: probs = [0.0158, 0.1171, 0.8649, 0.0021] for logits [2, 3, 4, 1], sorted by confidence logit4 > logit3 > logit2 > logit1.

// TOP-k = 2 — always keep a FIXED COUNT of 2, no matter how confident the model already is kept = { logit4: 0.8649, logit3: 0.1171} sum = 0.9820 renorm = { logit4: 0.8649/0.9820, logit3: 0.1171/0.9820} = [ 0.8808, 0.1192] dropped = {logit2, logit1} → forced to 0 // TOP-p = 0.85 — keep the SMALLEST set whose cumulative probability ≥ 0.85 cumulative: logit4 alone = 0.8649 → already ≥ 0.85, STOP kept = { logit4: 0.8649} sum = 0.8649 renorm = { logit4: 0.8649/0.8649} = [ 1.0000] ← nucleus = 1 token dropped = {logit3, logit2, logit1} → forced to 0 // same p = 0.85, applied instead to the flatter T = 0.7 distribution from above // (probs = [0.0438, 0.1828, 0.7629, 0.0105]): cumulative: logit4 = 0.7629 (< 0.85) → + logit3 = 0.9457 (≥ 0.85), STOP kept = { logit4: 0.7629, logit3: 0.1828} → renorm = [ 0.8067, 0.1933] ← nucleus = 2 tokens

Same k = 2 would have returned two candidates in both cases, whether the model was confident or not. Top-p = 0.85 didn't: on the peaked T = 0.5 distribution it needed only one token to clear the threshold; on the flatter T = 0.7 distribution it needed two. That's the adaptive behavior the callout below is about — top-p reads the shape of the distribution before deciding how many candidates are even in play.

Exam favourite: "What's the practical difference between top-k and top-p?" — top-k is a fixed candidate count that ignores confidence (it'll keep 3 tokens even if the model is 99% sure of one answer, or if it's genuinely torn between ten); top-p adapts to confidence by cutting on cumulative probability mass instead of a raw count, which is why most production APIs default to tuning top-p (and/or temperature) rather than top-k.

Why LLMs hallucinate, and how decoding + grounding reduce it

Hallucination isn't a bug that slipped through — it falls directly out of the mechanics above. Softmax always outputs a full probability distribution that sums to 1, whether or not the model actually has good evidence for any of the candidate tokens. There is no "none of the above" option built into the math — step 6 above always samples something. And because generation is autoregressive (Section 5), one unsupported token that gets sampled early becomes part of the input context for every token that follows — the model then generates a fluent, coherent-sounding continuation that justifies its own earlier mistake, rather than backtracking. Pretraining's objective (predict the next token in web text, Section 1) rewards fluency and plausibility, not verified truth — a confident wrong answer and a confident right answer are indistinguishable to the loss function that trained the model.

Mitigation What it actually does What it targets
Lower temperature / lower top-p Keeps sampling close to the model's highest-confidence path Random wandering into low-probability, unsupported continuations — not wrong knowledge itself
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) Grounds generation in retrieved, real documents instead of relying on parametric memory alone Gaps and errors in what the model "remembers" from pretraining
"Say you don't know" prompting / instruction-tuning Gives the model an actual token sequence to fall back to instead of confabulating The "softmax must output something" problem, directly
RLHF / calibration fine-tuning Rewards correctly expressed uncertainty over confident wrongness during training What the model considers a "good" continuation — not just how it's sampled at inference
Self-consistency (sample N times, compare) Disagreement across independent samples flags a likely-unsupported claim Catches hallucination after generation, at the cost of N× inference compute
Citation-forcing / attribution Requires every claim to point at a specific retrieved passage Makes a fabricated claim visibly ungrounded (no citation) instead of silently plausible
Exam favourite: "Does setting temperature to 0 eliminate hallucination?" — No. Temperature and top-p/top-k only control how the model samples among the tokens it already considers plausible; they cannot fix wrong or missing knowledge inside the model itself. Greedy decoding (T = 0) makes output fully deterministic and less "wandering," but if the model's single top-ranked token is a confidently wrong fact, greedy decoding will pick that same wrong fact every single time. Grounding (RAG) and calibration training address the actual knowledge/confidence gap; decoding parameters only address the sampling noise layered on top of it.
Section 06

Encoder-Only vs. Decoder-Only vs. Encoder–Decoder — Recap & When to Use Each

Module 4 introduced this split structurally. Here it is again, task-first — the question you should actually ask is "what shape is my task?", not "what architecture sounds fanciest."

E

Encoder-only

BERT, RoBERTa, DeBERTa. Bidirectional attention (no causal mask) — every token sees every other token, both directions. Trained with masked-language-modeling, not next-token prediction. Best for: classification, NER, embeddings/retrieval — anything that outputs a label or a vector, not free-form text.

D

Decoder-only

GPT-4, Claude, Llama, Gemini, Mistral, Phi, Gemma. Causal self-attention only, next-token-prediction objective, no cross-attention. Best for: open-ended generation — chat, coding, reasoning, summarization phrased as generation. This is the dominant architecture for modern LLMs.

ED

Encoder–Decoder

T5, BART, the original Transformer, Whisper. Separate bidirectional encoder + causal decoder with cross-attention. Best for: tasks with a clear input→output transformation of different structure — translation, speech-to-text — where the encoder can fully digest the input before generation starts.

Encoder-only Decoder-only Encoder–Decoder
Attention direction Bidirectional Causal (left-to-right) Bidirectional (encoder) + Causal (decoder)
Pretraining objective Masked LM (predict hidden tokens) Next-token prediction Span corruption / seq2seq
Can it generate free text? No Yes Yes
Natural output Label / embedding vector Token sequence Token sequence, grounded in a separate input
Why it's less common for LLMs today n/a — different use case entirely n/a — this is the mainstream choice Extra encoder cost isn't needed once everything is framed as "one sequence in, generate the rest"
The historical shift, in one sentence: the field moved from "pick the architecture that structurally matches your task" (2018-era BERT vs. GPT vs. T5) to "decoder-only can be prompted to do almost any task, so just scale that up" (2020s). This is exactly why decoder-only dominates the current LLM landscape.

Same task, two mechanisms: how GPT and T5 both translate "Hello world" into French

Both a decoder-only model like GPT and an encoder–decoder model like T5 can take Hello world and produce Bonjour le monde — but they get there through structurally different machinery, because they don't share an architecture. GPT has no encoder at all; T5 has nothing but an encoder feeding a decoder. Understanding how each one manages the same task is the fastest way to see what the architecture split from the previous section actually buys (or costs) you.

FIG 5 — Same prompt, same output, two different mechanisms. GPT keeps the instruction and the translation in one causal stream and just predicts forward. T5 fully digests the source sentence in a separate bidirectional encoder first, then a causal decoder cross-attends into that finished representation at every generation step.
G

How GPT translates

Architecture: one causal decoder stack, no encoder, no cross-attention. Pretraining objective: plain next-token prediction on raw text — translation is never a named training task. How the task is framed: the instruction and source sentence ("Translate to French: Hello world") are just the first tokens of the sequence; the model wasn't taught "translation," it learned — from seeing enough parallel text during pretraining/instruction-tuning — that this prompt shape is usually followed by a French continuation. Mechanism: at each step it predicts the next token conditioned on everything before it (prompt + whatever it has generated so far), via the KV-cached causal attention from Section 5. Nothing structurally distinguishes "reading the prompt" from "writing the answer" — it's all one stream.

T

How T5 translates

Architecture: a bidirectional encoder feeding a causal decoder through cross-attention (Section 6's Encoder–Decoder row). Pretraining objective: span corruption — mask out chunks of text and train the model to reconstruct them — then fine-tuned on explicit seq2seq pairs, with translation as one of many tasks selected by a text prefix (T5's "text-to-text" framing, e.g. "translate English to French: Hello world"). How the task is framed: the prefix routes the input to behave like a translation example the model was directly fine-tuned on, not an emergent pattern. Mechanism: the encoder reads "Hello world" bidirectionally — every source token sees every other source token, both directions — and produces a finished representation before generation starts. The decoder then generates "Bonjour le monde" causally, and at every single step also cross-attends into all of the encoder's finished output, not just what it has generated itself.

GPT (decoder-only) T5 (encoder–decoder)
Source-sentence attention Causal — later prompt tokens can't look ahead either; the whole prompt is processed left-to-right like everything else Bidirectional — full context of "Hello world" in both directions, finished before decoding starts
How the model reaches the output Same causal self-attention mechanism used for source and target — no separate pathway Decoder uses causal self-attention among its own tokens plus a separate cross-attention into the encoder's output
Where "translation" lives Implicitly, in patterns learned across the whole pretraining/instruction-tuning corpus Explicitly, as one of the seq2seq tasks selected by a training-time text prefix
Cross-attention? None — there is no second sequence to attend into Yes — the defining mechanism of the architecture
Exam favourite: "If T5's encoder gives it a structural advantage for translation, why do decoder-only LLMs like GPT-4 now translate just as well or better?" — scale and instruction-tuning close the gap: a large enough decoder-only model, trained on enough parallel and instruction data, learns the same "attend fully to the source before committing to output" behavior within its single causal stream — it just isn't forced to by the architecture the way T5's encoder forces it. T5's advantage was structural efficiency at a given size, not a capability decoder-only models are structurally incapable of reaching.
Section 07

Foundation Models — One Pretraining Run, Many Downstream Tasks

A foundation model is a model pretrained once, at large scale, on broad data, intended to be adapted — via prompting or fine-tuning (Module 6) — to many downstream tasks, rather than trained from scratch for each one. This is the economic and technical core of the modern LLM ecosystem.

Scaling laws: why bigger (done right) keeps helping

FIG 6 — Schematic view of the Chinchilla finding: loss keeps improving smoothly with compute only when parameter count and training-data volume scale together — a model with too many parameters for too little data plateaus early, wasting compute.
The Chinchilla result (Hoffmann et al., 2022): for a fixed compute budget, most earlier LLMs (GPT-3 included) were over-parameterized and under-trained — a smaller model trained on proportionally more tokens reaches lower loss for the same compute. Rule of thumb from that paper: roughly 20 training tokens per parameter is compute-optimal.

Emergent abilities

Some capabilities — multi-step arithmetic, chain-of-thought reasoning, in-context learning itself — appear to show up fairly abruptly past a certain scale threshold, rather than improving smoothly. This is one of the more debated claims in the field: some of it may be a genuine phase transition in capability, some of it may be a measurement artifact of using metrics (like exact-match accuracy) that are themselves discontinuous. Either way, it's the empirical reason scale kept being pushed even after diminishing returns on perplexity alone.

Pretrain → adapt: the two-stage recipe

Stage 1 — Pretraining
  • Next-token prediction on trillions of tokens of broad text/code
  • Extremely expensive: weeks on thousands of GPUs
  • Produces a "base model" — fluent, knowledgeable, not yet obedient
  • Done once, by a handful of labs (OpenAI, Meta, Google, Anthropic, Mistral, ...)
Stage 2 — Adaptation
  • Instruction tuning, RLHF/DPO (turns base model into a chat assistant)
  • Task-specific fine-tuning — full or parameter-efficient (Module 6)
  • In-context learning / prompting — no weight updates at all
  • Cheap enough for individual teams, sometimes a single GPU
Section 08

Open-Source vs. Closed-Source Models

"Open-source LLM" is a loaded term — most released weights come with usage restrictions that don't meet the strict open-source definition. The practically useful axis is: can you download and run the weights yourself, or only call an API?

Open-weight Closed / API-only
Examples Llama 3/4, Mistral, Gemma, Phi, DeepSeek, Qwen GPT-4/5, Claude, Gemini (API-served versions)
Deployment Self-host: your infra, your GPUs (or CPU, for small models) Vendor's infra only, via API
Data privacy Full control — nothing leaves your environment Data sent to a third party (subject to their policy)
Fine-tuning Full access to weights → full fine-tuning or PEFT (Module 6) Only vendor-provided fine-tuning APIs, if offered, on their terms
Cost model Upfront/ongoing infra cost, no per-token fee Pay-per-token, zero infra to manage
Frontier capability Narrowing gap, but closed models often lead on hardest benchmarks Typically at or near the capability frontier
Licensing Varies — Llama's license has usage-scale restrictions; Apache 2.0 (Mistral, some Qwen) is truly permissive Commercial terms of service, not a software license
Why this course fine-tunes an open-weight model: fine-tuning (Module 6) requires access to the weights themselves — you cannot LoRA-tune a model you can only call through an API. Small open-weight models (Phi, Gemma, Flan-T5) are specifically chosen because they're capable enough to be useful, and small enough to fine-tune without a data-center GPU.

Choosing between them — a practical checklist

?

Data sensitivity

Regulated/private data → strong pull toward self-hosted open-weight models.

Section 09

Key Takeaways & Exam Prep

✓

LLMs are one objective, scaled up

Next-token prediction, self-supervised, on trillions of tokens. Every downstream capability sits on top of this.

✓

Tokenization shapes behavior

Subword schemes (BPE/WordPiece/Unigram) balance vocabulary size against sequence length — and explain real model quirks.

✓

Embeddings encode meaning geometrically

A learned lookup table where direction and distance carry semantic relationships.

✓

RoPE is the modern positional default

Rotates Q/K by position; scores depend on relative distance, not absolute position.

✓

Decoder-only dominates

Causal masking + KV-caching is the architecture behind essentially every LLM you talk to.

✓

Scale must be balanced

Chinchilla: parameters and training tokens must grow together, or compute is wasted.

✓

Foundation models separate pretrain from adapt

One expensive pretraining run, many cheap downstream adaptations — the subject of Module 6.

✓

Open weights are a prerequisite for fine-tuning

You cannot LoRA-tune what you can only call through an API.

Common exam questions

Question Short answer
What is the pretraining objective for a decoder-only LLM? Autoregressive next-token prediction — maximize P(token_t | token_<t)
Why subword tokenization instead of word- or character-level? Bounded vocabulary, no <UNK> tokens, and much shorter sequences than character-level for the same text
What does RoPE rotate, and why? Query and Key vectors, by an angle proportional to position — so attention scores depend only on relative distance
Why is a causal mask needed during training but not at the embedding layer? It prevents a position from attending to future tokens during the parallel forward pass, which would let training "see the answer"
What is a KV cache and why does it matter? Stores previously computed Key/Value vectors during generation so they aren't recomputed every step — turns O(n²) generation into O(n)
What did the Chinchilla paper show? For fixed compute, model size and training-token count should scale together (~20 tokens/parameter) — many earlier LLMs were over-sized relative to their training data
Why is almost every modern LLM decoder-only rather than encoder–decoder? Prompting can reframe most tasks as one sequence to continue, removing the need for a separate encoder + cross-attention
