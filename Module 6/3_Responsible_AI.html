Section 01

Fairness

Fairness asks whether a model's outputs treat different groups equitably — and, crucially, "equitably" has more than one formal definition, which can point to different, sometimes incompatible, conclusions about the same system.

Fairness definitionWhat it requires
Demographic parityOutcomes (e.g. approval rate) are statistically similar across groups, regardless of other differences between them
Equalized oddsError rates (false positive/negative rates) are similar across groups, conditioned on the true outcome
Individual fairnessSimilar individuals receive similar outcomes, regardless of group membership
Exam favourite: "Can a system satisfy demographic parity and equalized odds simultaneously?" — Generally not, except in special cases (e.g. equal base rates across groups). This is a proven mathematical result, not an engineering shortfall — choosing a fairness definition is a values decision with real trade-offs, not a technical problem with one correct answer.
Section 02

Bias

Bias in an LLM is systematic skew in outputs correlated with protected or sensitive attributes — and, given Module 1 §01's core mechanism (next-token prediction on internet-scale text), it enters overwhelmingly through training data, not through any explicit rule anyone wrote.

Bias sourceMechanism
Training data imbalanceUnder/over-representation of groups, dialects, or viewpoints in the pretraining corpus
Historical bias in dataThe internet reflects existing societal biases; a model trained to predict "what comes next" reproduces those patterns faithfully
Annotator/labeler biasInstruction-tuning and RLHF data (Module 1 §07) reflects the judgments and blind spots of whoever labeled it
Evaluation biasBenchmarks themselves can be skewed toward particular languages, cultures, or demographics, masking bias that a broader evaluation would reveal
Why bias is genuinely hard to "fix" rather than just detect: Module 1 §01 established that an LLM's fluency and knowledge come entirely from patterns in training data — the same mechanism that produces useful capability also faithfully reproduces whatever skew exists in that data. There's no separate "bias module" to patch; mitigation means changing data composition, training objectives, or adding explicit post-hoc correction layers, each with its own trade-offs against capability and cost.
Section 03

Privacy

Two distinct privacy risks apply to LLM systems, easy to conflate but requiring different mitigations:

RiskMechanismMitigation
Training data memorizationA model can memorize and later regurgitate specific snippets from its training data, including personal information that was present in itData de-duplication and PII scrubbing before training; differential privacy techniques during training
Runtime data exposureUser inputs (Module 6 Part 2 §01's context window) may be logged, used for further training, or exposed via prompt injection/leakage (Part 2 §01, §04)Clear data retention policies, opt-out mechanisms, not training on user data by default without consent
Connects directly to Module 6 Part 2: system prompt leakage (Part 2 §04) is a privacy risk when the leaked system prompt itself contains sensitive business logic; indirect prompt injection (Part 2 §02) can be weaponized specifically to exfiltrate private data an agent has access to. Privacy and security are separate concerns but share the same underlying attack surfaces.
Section 04

Explainability

Explainability asks: can you say why a model produced a given output? For LLMs, this is genuinely harder than for simpler models — a decision tree's reasoning is inspectable directly; a Transformer's next-token prediction (Module 1 §03) emerges from billions of interacting parameters with no single "reason" to point to.

ApproachWhat it providesLimitation
Attention visualizationWhich tokens a layer attended to most (Module 1 §03's attention weights)Attention weight is not the same as causal explanation — high attention doesn't prove that input caused the output
Chain-of-thought as explanationThe model's own stated reasoning (Module 2 §03)A model's stated reasoning isn't guaranteed to reflect its actual computation — it can be a plausible-sounding post-hoc rationalization
Feature attribution methodsWhich parts of the input most influenced the output, via perturbation or gradient-based analysisComputationally expensive at LLM scale; results can be unstable or hard to interpret
Exam favourite: "Does chain-of-thought reasoning make an LLM's decisions explainable?" — Not reliably. CoT (Module 2 §03) improves accuracy by giving the model more computation, but the generated reasoning trace is not guaranteed to be a faithful account of the actual internal computation that produced the answer — it's the model's best guess at a plausible explanation, generated by the same process as the answer itself, not a window into it.
Section 05

Governance

AI governance is the organizational and regulatory structure around how AI systems get built, deployed, and monitored — the practices that turn SS01-SS04's principles into enforceable process rather than good intentions.

Governance layerWhat it covers
Internal policyWhat data can train models, what use cases require review, who signs off before deployment
Documentation & transparencyModel cards, data sheets — recording what a model was trained on, evaluated for, and known to be limited by
Regulatory complianceExternal requirements (e.g. the EU AI Act's risk-tiered obligations) that vary by jurisdiction and use case
Ongoing monitoringGovernance doesn't end at launch — Module 6 Part 4's observability is what makes ongoing governance actually enforceable rather than aspirational
The throughline connecting this entire Part: fairness, bias, privacy, and explainability are all measurement problems before they're policy problems — you cannot govern what you haven't defined a way to check. Module 6 Part 1's evaluation frameworks and Part 4's observability are the practical machinery that makes responsible-AI principles actually verifiable in a running system, not just a document nobody checks against.

Key Takeaways & Exam Prep

✓

Fairness has multiple, sometimes incompatible definitions

Choosing one is a values decision, not a purely technical one.

✓

Bias enters mainly through data, not explicit rules

The same mechanism that gives LLMs capability also faithfully reproduces training-data skew.

✓

Privacy has two distinct risk types

Training-data memorization vs. runtime data exposure need different mitigations.

✓

Chain-of-thought is not proof of reasoning

Stated reasoning can be a plausible rationalization, not a faithful account of actual computation.

✓

Governance requires measurement first

Principles become enforceable only once there's a way to check them — Module 6 Parts 1 and 4's job.
